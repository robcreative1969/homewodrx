Why the First One Takes So Long

The strict pull-up is one of the few movements where you either can or you cannot. There is no half credit. You can grind out a heavy squat with ugly form, but if your back and arms cannot move your bodyweight from a dead hang to chin over the bar, nothing happens. You just hang there.

That all-or-nothing quality is what makes it so discouraging. Most people try a few times, fail, and conclude they are not built for it. The real issue is usually simpler: they have never trained the pieces of the movement on their own, so there is nothing to build on.

The good news is that the pieces are easy to train at home with a bar and a little patience. The honest news is that it takes weeks to months, not days. Plan for that and you will get there.

Find Your Starting Point

Before picking a progression, run three quick tests on a fresh day:

  • Dead hang. Hang from the bar with straight arms and time it. Under 20 seconds means grip is going to be a limiter.
  • Inverted row. Set a bar or rings around hip height, walk your feet out, and row your chest up. Count clean reps with a straight body.
  • Negative. Jump or step to the top position, chin over the bar, and lower as slowly as you can. Time the descent.

If you can hang for 30 seconds, do 10 or more solid rows with your body close to horizontal, and control a negative for 5 seconds, you are closer than you think. If any of those fall short, that is where your early work goes. Write the numbers down. You will want them in a month.

The Progression, Piece by Piece

These build on each other, but you do not have to finish one before starting the next. Most sessions should include two or three of them.

Scapular pulls. From a dead hang, pull your shoulder blades down and back without bending your elbows. Your body rises an inch or two. This is the very first part of every pull-up, and a surprising number of people skip it and try to pull with their arms alone. Sets of 8 to 10.

Inverted rows. Your main strength builder early on. Make them harder by walking your feet forward until your body is closer to parallel with the floor, and pause with your chest at the bar for a second on each rep.

Negatives. Start at the top and lower yourself over 3 to 5 seconds. Stop the set when you can no longer control the speed. These are demanding, so keep the volume modest: 3 to 5 reps, a few sets, and leave a day between sessions.

Band-assisted pull-ups. A resistance band looped over the bar takes some bodyweight off at the bottom, which is where most people are weakest. A set of bands in a few thicknesses lets you step down gradually. The catch is that bands help most at the bottom and least at the top, so pair them with negatives rather than using bands alone.

Flexed-arm hang. Hold the top position, chin over the bar, for as long as you can. It builds strength at exactly the point where band work leaves a gap.

Practical habit: If the bar is somewhere you walk past every day, hang from it or do a couple of scap pulls each time. Small, frequent exposure adds up faster than one long session, as long as you never go near failure.

A Simple Weekly Plan

Three short sessions a week is enough. Put them at the start of a workout, when you are fresh, and keep each one to about 15 minutes.

  • Day 1: Scap pulls, 3 sets of 8. Inverted rows, 4 sets of 8. Flexed-arm hang, 3 holds.
  • Day 2: Negatives, 4 sets of 3. Dead hang, 3 sets as long as you can.
  • Day 3: Band-assisted pull-ups, 4 sets of 5. Inverted rows, 3 sets of 10.

Every week, make one thing slightly harder. A thinner band, a slower negative, feet a little further forward on the rows. Retest your hang, rows, and negative every four weeks. When a negative lasts 8 to 10 seconds and you can hold the top for 15 or more, try a strict rep at the start of a session. Plenty of people are surprised by how that attempt goes.

Pull-Ups in Your WODs While You Build

Pull-ups show up in a lot of benchmark workouts, and you do not need to skip those while you work toward your first one. You just need a substitution that keeps the pulling in the workout.

Inverted rows are the best stand-in for most people, because you can do them fast and keep the workout moving. Band-assisted reps work if the band is light enough that you are still working and heavy enough that you are not stalling after three reps. Jumping pull-ups with a slow lowering are fine in small doses, but they get sloppy in high-rep workouts.

A workout like Cindy is a good place to practice this, since the pull-up sets are small. The Cindy pacing guide covers how to scale it without losing the point.

One thing to hold off on: kipping pull-ups. The kip is a useful skill, but it is built on top of strict strength, not in place of it. Get a few strict reps first.

Final Thoughts

The first pull-up is mostly a patience test. Train the pieces three times a week, make each session a little harder than the last, and retest on a schedule so you can see it coming.

When it finally happens, it usually happens on a day you were not expecting much. Write it down. Then go after the second one.
